# Word of Mouth, on Purpose

Most consultancies say they grow by referral, but few make referrals *easy*. Word of mouth isn't passive. It's a system, just a quiet one.

---

## 1. Make it easy to describe us

People refer what they can explain in one breath. Give them the words.

**The one-liner** (what we want people to say at dinner):
> "They're the people you call when your operations are a mess. Really good, really honest, and they won't try to sell you AI you don't need."

**The forwardable paragraph** (send this to anyone who offers an intro):
> Custodial Arts helps digital businesses clean up operations, tools, and technology: the handoffs, processes, and software sprawl that slow everything down. They start by measuring how things work today, fix what's actually broken, and report results in real numbers. They're also refreshingly honest about AI: what's ready to use now, what isn't, and what needs cleaning up first. [Name] is great to talk to even if you're just trying to figure out whether you have a problem.

---

## 2. Know who refers

Referrals come from four places. Treat each one differently.

| Source | Why they refer | How to stay present |
|---|---|---|
| **Past clients** | They saw the results | Night Shift retainer, a quarterly check-in call, and sending them useful things with no ask attached |
| **Ops people at past clients** | You made their job easier and listened to them | Stay in touch when they change jobs. They'll bring you to the next company. |
| **Adjacent pros** (fractional CFOs, recruiters, agencies, lawyers, accountants) | You make them look good when they refer you | Refer work back. Get coffee twice a year. |
| **Former colleagues** | They know your work | Tell them clearly what you do now, once. Then be visible, not pushy. |

The ops people at past clients are probably the most valuable group. They move companies, and when they do, they bring the people who helped them.

---

## 3. Ask at the right moment

The best time to ask for a referral is right after a measurable win, when the client is looking at the before/after numbers.

> "Glad this worked. If you know anyone dealing with something similar, I'd be grateful for an intro. No pressure, and happy to talk to them even if it's just to point them in the right direction."

Ask once. Don't make it a campaign.

---

## 4. Close the loop

- **Thank every referrer**, whether or not the work happens. A handwritten note stands out.
- **Tell them what happened** (with the client's permission): "We ended up working together, and it went well. Thank you."
- **Take good care of their referral.** The referrer's reputation is on the line, not just yours.
- **Refer generously.** When something isn't a fit, send them to someone good. It's the right thing to do, and it comes back around.

---

## 5. Things that help without being advertising

- **The Condition Report is portable.** Make the Walkthrough deliverable good enough that clients forward it internally and to peers.
- **Case notes.** Write a short anonymized before/after for every engagement with the client's sign-off, and use them in conversations.
- **A simple site.** Something credible to land on when someone looks you up after an intro (see `/site`).
- **LinkedIn presence.** Where people check you out before replying to an intro (see [linkedin.md](../content/linkedin.md)).

---

## Simple tracking

A spreadsheet is fine. Yes, we see the irony. Give it an owner.

| Date | Referred by | Referred who | Outcome | Thanked? | Updated referrer? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Review it monthly. The goal isn't volume. It's to know who is sending work, so you can thank them and take care of the relationship.
