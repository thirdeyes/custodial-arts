# Content Playbook: LinkedIn & Professional Channels

No ads or campaigns. Content has one job: when someone hears "you should talk to Custodial Arts," the profile they look up should confirm it. Every post should be useful to a reader who never hires us.

---

## Ground rules

1. **Useful first, funny second, promotional never.** If a post only works as an ad, cut it.
2. **Anonymize everything.** No client names, no identifiable details, no screenshots of real systems. Change the industry if it'd give it away. Get permission before telling anything recognizable.
3. **Punch at the mess, never at people.** (See [voice.md](../brand/voice.md).)
4. **Real numbers or no numbers.** Never invent a statistic for a post.
5. **One wink per post.** The custodial joke is seasoning, not the meal.
6. **Keep it short.** Most posts land between 80 and 200 words. Short lines, no walls of text.

## Cadence

Aim for 1–2 posts a week. Being consistent matters more than being frequent. A good post every Tuesday beats five mediocre ones and then a month of silence.

## Recurring formats

| Series | What it is | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| **From the Permanent Collection** | A common mess written up like a museum placard: title, date, medium, description. Then a plain-English fix. | Every other week |
| **Condition Report** | A short anonymized story: what we found, what we did, what moved (in numbers), and what didn't. | Monthly |
| **AI Claim, Inspected** | Take a real, public AI claim (vendor marketing, a headline). Say what's true, what's missing, and what it would take to work in a normal business. Critique the claim, not the company or its people. | Every other week |
| **Supply Closet Inventory** | Practical checklists: "5 questions to ask before you buy another tool." | Monthly |
| **Notes from the Night Shift** | Short observations. One line, one idea. | As they come |

## Starter posts

Drafts ready to adapt. Swap in real experiences wherever you have them. The more specific, the better.

### 1. Launch post

> I'm starting a consultancy called Custodial Arts.
>
> The name is a joke about calling a janitor "an expert in the custodial arts." It's also the whole idea.
>
> Every business runs on unglamorous work: the handoffs, the reports someone rebuilds by hand every Monday, the tool nobody remembers buying. That work decides whether things actually run. It rarely gets expert attention because it's not exciting.
>
> So that's what we do. Operations, tools, and technology for digital businesses. The messes nobody wants to touch.
>
> A few house rules:
> → We measure before we mop. Baseline first, results in real numbers.
> → We don't oversell. 12% is 12%.
> → Your building, your rules. We work the way you work.
> → We'll tell you when you don't need us.
>
> Most of our work will come from people who know people. If you know someone standing in a mess, I'd be grateful for the intro.

### 2. From the Permanent Collection: The Spreadsheet

> **Untitled (The Spreadsheet Nobody Owns)**
> 2019–present
> Excel, email attachments, one person's memory
>
> *This work began as a quick fix. It now has 43 tabs, three versions called FINAL, and powers the board report. Its creator left in 2022. Nobody dares sort column F.*
>
> Every company has one. It's not a failure, it's proof someone solved a real problem with what they had.
>
> The fix usually isn't new software. It's:
> 1. Find out who actually uses it and for what
> 2. Give it an owner
> 3. Kill the tabs nobody reads (usually more than half)
> 4. *Then* decide if it needs to become something else
>
> Where's yours?

### 3. AI Claim, Inspected

> "Our AI agent handles 80% of support tickets autonomously."
>
> Maybe! Questions worth asking before you sign:
>
> → 80% of *whose* tickets? The demo set or yours?
> → What counts as "handled"? Closed, or resolved?
> → What happens to the 20%, and how fast do they reach a human?
> → Who fixes it when the AI confidently gives the wrong answer?
> → What does your knowledge base look like? The AI can only be as right as the docs it reads.
>
> That last one is where most pilots stall. It's not glamorous, and it's the actual work.
>
> A great new mop. Someone still has to know where the floor is.

### 4. Measure before you mop

> Before we change anything, we write down how things work today, in numbers.
>
> How long does onboarding take? How many hours a week go into the Monday report? How many times does an order get touched by hand?
>
> Clients sometimes want to skip this. It feels slow. But without a baseline:
> → You can't tell if the fix worked
> → You can't tell if it was worth what you paid
> → Everyone remembers the "before" as better or worse than it was
>
> Also, the baseline alone often surprises people. "We spend *how* many hours on that?"

### 5. The frontline knows

> The fastest way to find out what's broken in a company is to ask the person who has to work around it every day.
>
> They know which report is wrong and which field everyone ignores. They know the workaround and why it exists. They've usually known for a year.
>
> Nobody asked them. Or someone asked, and nothing happened.
>
> We start every engagement there. It's not a technique. It's just where the information is.

### 6. Notes from the Night Shift (one-liners)

> Every "quick fix" in your stack is someone's permanent job now.

> If only one person can explain how it works, that's not a process. It's a hostage situation.

> The best tool is the one your team actually opens.

> "We'll clean up the data after we launch the AI" is the "I'll start the diet Monday" of enterprise software.

> Nobody has ever been promoted for fixing the handoff between sales and ops. It's still the most valuable thing in the building.

### 7. When you don't need us

> Told a prospective client this week they didn't need to hire us.
>
> Their ops lead had already diagnosed the problem, had a plan, and just needed two uninterrupted weeks to do it.
>
> So that was our recommendation: clear the calendar, back the plan, check in at the end of the month.
>
> Part of doing this work honestly is saying so when the fix is already in the building.

*(Only post this one if it actually happened. That rule applies to every story here.)*

### 8. Supply Closet Inventory: before you buy another tool

> Five questions before adding a tool to your stack:
>
> 1. What does it replace? (If "nothing," be suspicious.)
> 2. Who owns it after the person who bought it moves on?
> 3. Where does its data go, and who's responsible for keeping it right?
> 4. What happens to the workflow if it goes down for a day?
> 5. In 12 months, how will you know if it was worth it?
>
> If you can't answer #5 today, you won't be able to answer it in 12 months either.

## Other outlets

- **Newsletter (optional, later):** A monthly "Condition Report" email to past clients and referrers. Low effort, keeps you in the room when referrals happen.
- **Podcasts & panels:** Say yes to ops, RevOps, and founder-focused shows. The contrarian AI angle gets bookings.
- **Guest posts:** Industry newsletters for operators, not marketers.
- **Talks:** "The Permanent Collection," a talk of museum placards for common business messes. It works as a talk, a post series, and eventually a small book.
